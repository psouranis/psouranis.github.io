In this post, we’ll take a deep dive into one of the most fascinating intersections of machine learning and mathematics. Bayesian optimization is a class of machine-learning-based optimization methods focused on solving the problem

\[\max_{x \in A} f(x)\]

where the feasible set and objective function typically have the following properties:

  • The input \(x\in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) for a value of \(d\) that is not too large. Typically \(d \leq 20\) in most successful applications of Bayesian Optimization.
  • The feasible set \(A\) is a simple set, in which it is easy to assess membership. Typically \(A\) is a hyper-rectangle \(\big\{x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}: a_i \leq x_i \leq b_i\big\}\) or the \(d\)-dimensional simplex \(\big\{x \in \mathbb{R}^{d} : \sum_i x_i = 1\big\}\).
  • The objective function \(f\) is continuous. This will typically be required to model \(f\) using Gaussian process regression.
  • \(f\) is “expensive to evaluate” in the sense that the number of evaluations that may be performed is limited, typically to a few hundred. This limitation typically arises because each evaluation takes a substantial amount of time (typically hours), but may also occur because each evaluation bears a monetary cost or an opportunity cost.
  • \(f\) lacks known special structure like concativity or linearity that would make it easy to optimize using techniques that leverage such structure to improve efficiency.
  • When we evaluate \(f\), we observe only \(f(x)\) and no first or second derivatives. This prevents the application of first and second order methods like gradient descent, Newton’s method, or quasi-Newton methods.
  • Our focus is on finding a global rather than local optimum.

Methodology

Bayesian optimization consists of \(2\) main components: a Bayesian statistical model for modelling the objective function, and an acquisition function for deciding where to sample next. The procedure is described in the following algorithm

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Basic pseudo-code for Bayesian optimization}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE Place a Gaussian process prior on \(f\).
\STATE Observe \(f\) at \(i_0\) points according to an initial space-filling experimental design. Set \(i=i_{0}\).
\While {\(i \leq\) N}
    \STATE Update the posterior probability distribution on \(f\) using all available data.
    \STATE Let \(x\)$_{i}$ be a maximizer of the acquisition function over \(x\), where the acquisition function is computed using the current posterior distribution.
    \STATE Observe y\(_{i}=f({x_{i}})\).
    \STATE Increment \(i\).
\EndWhile
\STATE Return a solution: either the point evaluated with the largest \(f(x)\), or the point with the largest posterior mean.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We shall see next, how this is translated to some basic Python code but first let’s examine what is a Gaussian process and how we can fit it to the data.

Gaussian Processes

Definition 1. (Gaussian Process).
A Gaussian process is a collection of random variables, any finite number of which have a joint Gaussian Distribution. A Gaussian process is completely specified by its mean function and covariance function. We define mean function \(m(x)\) and the covariance functiton \(k'(x, x')\) of a real process \(f(x)\) as $$ \begin{split} m(x) &= \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\big]\\ k(x, x') &= \mathbb{E}\big[\big(f(x) - m(x)\big)\big(f(x') - m(x')\big)\big] \end{split} $$ and will write the Gaussian process as $$ f(x) \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(m(x), k(x,x')\big) $$


We first describe Gaussian Process regression, focusing on \(f\)’s values at a finite collection of points \(x_1, \ldots , x_k \in \mathbb{R}^d\). It is convenient to call the collection of these values as \(x_{1:k}\) and the collection of the function’s values evaluated at those points as \(f(x_{1:k}) = \big[f(x_1), f(x_2), \ldots, f(x_k)]\).

A simple example of a Gaussian process can be obtained from our Bayesian linear regression model \(y = f(x) = x^{\top}w + \varepsilon,\) where \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{k}^{2})\) and \(w \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\). We use \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{k}^{2})\) to denote that the output might be corrupted by some Gaussian noise.

In the Bayesian formalism we also need to specify a prior over the parameters \(w\), expressing our beliefs about the prior parameters before we look at the observation. We most commonly use a Gaussian prior with covariance matrix \(\Sigma_{0}\) on the weights \(w \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{0}, \Sigma_{0})\). We use subscript \(0\) to denote that our initial beliefs for the mean and covariance. Viewing this as a Gaussian process in the function space we will have for the mean and covariance function

\[\begin{split} m(x) &= \mathbb{E}\big[f(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}[x^{\top}w + \varepsilon] = \mathbb{E}\big[x^{\top}w] + \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = \mathbb{E}\big[x^{\top}w] = x^{\top} \mathbb{E}[w] = x^{\top} \mu_{0} \quad (1)\\ k(x, x') &= \mathbb{E}\big[(f(x)-m(x))(f(x')-m(x'))\big] = x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x' \quad (2) \end{split}\]

Where \(k(x, x')\) the covariance function and \(m(x)\) the mean function. \(k(x, x')\) is also referred as Gram matrix or Kernel in some textbooks. The kernel is generally chosen so that points \(x_i, x_j\) that are closer in the input space have a large positive correlation, encoding the belief that they should have more similar function values than points that are far apart. In our case the kernel naturally arises from the definition of our bayesian linear regression.

So \(f(x)\) and \(f(x')\) are jointly Gaussian with mean and covariance given by equations (1) and (2) above.

\[f(x) \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(x^{\top}\mu_{0}, x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x'\big) \quad \text{ or equivalently } \quad \begin{bmatrix} f(x) \\ f(x') \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N} \bigg(\begin{bmatrix} x^{\top}\mu_{0} \\ x'^{\top}\mu_{0} \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} k(x, x) & k(x, x') \\ k(x', x) & k(x', x') \end{bmatrix}\bigg)\]

In essence, a Gaussian process arises naturally once we put a Gaussian prior on the weights \(w\) of our linear regression model. The weights prior covariance \(\Sigma_{0}\) dictates how much the weights are expected to vary, and this directly translates into how “similar” function values are expected to be at different input points, as captured by the kernel function \(k(x,x')\).

Suppose now that we wish to infer the value of \(f(x_{k+1})\) in a new data point \(x_{k+1}\). From the definition we gave above for our Bayesian linear regression model, it follows that the collection \([f(x_{k+1}), f(x_{1:k})]\) will be a Gaussian process which has a joint Gaussian distribution. Since the collection has a joint Gaussian distribution, the conditional \(f(x_{k+1}) | f(x_{1:k})\) will also be a Gaussian according to Theorem 1 in Appendix.

\[f(x_{k+1}) | f(x_{1:k}) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{k}, \Sigma_{k})\]

where

\[\begin{equation} \tag{3} \begin{split} \mu_{k} &= x_{k+1}^{\top}\mu_{0} + \bigg(x_{k+1}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k}(x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k})^{-1}\bigg)(f(x_{1:k}) - x_{1:k}^{\top}\mu_{0}), \quad \mu_{k} \in \mathbb{R}\\ \Sigma_{k} &= x_{k+1}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{k+1} - \bigg(x_{k+1}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k}(x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k})^{-1}x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{k+1}\bigg), \quad \Sigma_{k} \in \mathbb{R} \end{split} \end{equation}\]

This conditional distribution is called the posterior probability distribution.

Rather than computing posterior means and variances directly using the equation above and matrix inversion, it is typically faster and more numerically stable to use a Cholesky decomposition and then solve a linear system of equations.
Additionally, to improve the numerical stability of this approach or direct computation using , it is often useful to add a small positive number like \(10^{−6}\) to each element of the diagonal of \(x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k}\) especially when \(x_{1:k}\) contains two or more points that are close together. This prevents the eigenvalues from being too close to \(0\), and only changes the predictions be made by a very small amount.

In this example we reviewed the Bayesian linear model which suffers from limited expressiveness. What we usually do in this case to overcome this problem is to first project the inputs into some high dimensional space using a set of basis functions and then apply the linear model in this space instead of directly on the inputs themselves.

For example, a scalar input \(x\) could be projected into the space of powers of \(x: \phi(x) = (1, x, x^{2}, x^{3}, \ldots, )^{\top}\) to implement polynomial regression. The only that will change in our formulation above will be that instead of \(x\) we will now have \(\phi(x)\) and our model predictions are given by \(y = f(x) = \phi(x)^\top w + \varepsilon\).

Choosing a Kernel

Kernels typically have the property that points closer in the input space are more strongly correlated, i.e., that if \(\left\Vert x - x' \right\Vert < \left\Vert x - x'' \right\Vert\) for some norm \(\left\Vert \cdot \right\Vert\), then \(k(x,x') > k(x, x'')\). Additionally, kernels are required to be positive semi-definite functions. In our simple linear regression example, the kernel arised naturally from the prior distribution over the weights as \(x^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x'\) (also called Linear Kernel).

Since \(\Sigma_{0}\) must be positive semi-definite (for invertibility), and this kernel satisfies that requirement, it fulfills all the necessary criteria. Other commonly used kernels are the power exponential or Gaussian kernel and the Matern kernel:

\[\begin{split} \text{(Power Exponential Gaussian)} \quad k(x, x') &= \alpha_{0}\exp(- {\left\Vert x - x' \right\Vert}^{2}), \\ \text{(Matern)} \quad k(x, x') &= \alpha_{0}\dfrac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\bigg(\sqrt{2\nu}{\left\Vert x - x' \right\Vert}^{2}\bigg)K_{\nu}(\sqrt{2\nu}\left\Vert x - x'\right\Vert) \end{split}\]

where \(\alpha_{0}\) are the hyperparameters of the kernel, \(K_{\nu}\) is the modified Bessel function and \(\nu\) is a positive parameter of the covariance.

Parameter Estimation

A very common method for the optimization of the hyperparameters is by means of the negative log marginal likelihood function, often simply named as (negative log) likelihood function. It is marginal since it is obtained through marginalization over the function \(f_{\text{GP}}\). The marginal likelihood is the likelihood that the output data \(y_{1:k}\) fits to the input data \(x_{1:k}\) with the hyperparameters \(\theta\). In the simple case of the bayesian linear regression that we outlined above we will have \(\theta = (\mu_{0}, \Sigma_{0}, \sigma_{k}^{2})\).

The marginal likelihood or evidence is the integral (or product in our case) of the likelihood times the Gaussian prior under our Gaussian process model.

\[p(y_{1:k}|x_{1:k}) = \prod_{i=1}^{k}p(y_{i} | f(x_{i}))p(f(x_{i}))\]

We can also easily observe that from the definition of our problem, \(y_{1:k} \vert x_{1:k}\) can be written as a sum of 2 Gaussian distributions

\[y_{1:k} | x_{1:k} = \mathcal{N}(x_{1:k}^{\top}\mu_{0}, x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k}) + \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{k}^{2}I)\]

According to Theorem 2 in Appendix we will have

\[y_{1:k} | x_{1:k} = \mathcal{N}(x_{1:k}^{\top}\mu_{0}, x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k} + \sigma_{k}^{2}I)\]

So our marginal is a Gaussian distribution. From Definition 1 in Appendix we end up with the (Negative Log Marginal Likelihood)

\[\begin{equation} \tag{4} -\ln L(\theta, y_{1:k} | x_{1:k}) = \dfrac{1}{2}(y_{1:k} - x_{1:k}^{\top}\mu_{0})^{\top}(x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k} + \sigma_{k}^{2}I)^{-1}(y_{1:k} - x_{1:k}^{\top}\mu_{0}) + \dfrac{1}{2}\ln\big(\vert x_{1:k}^{\top}\Sigma_{0}x_{1:k} + \sigma_{k}^{2}I\vert\big) + \dfrac{k}{2}\ln 2\pi \end{equation}\]

Since an analytic solution of the derivation is impossible, a gradient based optimization algorithm is typically used to minimize the function. However, the negative log likelihood is non-convex in general such that there is no guarantee to find the optimum \(\theta^{*}\).

Summary of Gaussian Process

We can summarize what we mentioned above regarding the Gaussian Process in the 3 fundamental steps:

1. Define the Prior Distribution over the functions. This step involves choosing a mean function \(m(x)\) and its covariance function \(k(x,x')\).
2. Use gradient descent to optimize the hyperparameters of the Gaussian Process by minimizing the negative log marginal likelihood (eg. 4). Use Adam or any other optimizer.
3. Calculate the predictive mean and predictive covariance for new data (eq. 3).

* For calculating the inverse matrix \(K^{-1}\) use Cholesky decomposition

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import torch
import gpytorch
import math

from numbers import Number
from math import pi
from torch import Tensor

class ExactGPModel(gpytorch.models.ExactGP):
    def __init__(self, train_x, train_y, likelihood):
        super(ExactGPModel, self).__init__(train_x, train_y, likelihood)
        self.mean_module = gpytorch.means.LinearMean(input_size=(1))
        self.covar_module = gpytorch.kernels.ScaleKernel(gpytorch.kernels.LinearKernel())

    def forward(self, x):
        mean_x = self.mean_module(x)
        covar_x = self.covar_module(x)
        return gpytorch.distributions.MultivariateNormal(mean_x, covar_x)

def train(model, likelihood, train_x, train_y, training_iter=1):

    model.train()
    likelihood.train()

    # Includes Gaussian Likelihood parameters
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.1)

    # The marginal log likelihood
    mll = gpytorch.mlls.ExactMarginalLogLikelihood(likelihood, model)

    for i in range(training_iter):
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        output = model(train_x)
        loss = -mll(output, train_y)
        loss.backward()

        optimizer.step()
    return model

This model is equivalent to a Bayesian linear regression with an isotropic Gaussian prior on the weights, \(w\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{d}^{2}I)\). This prior assumes the weights are independent and share a common variance (\(\sigma_{d}^{2}\)), which results in the following linear kernel:

\[k(x, x') = \sigma_{d}^{2}x^{\top}x'\]

More information can be found here: GPytorch: Linear Kernel.

Acquisition Functions

Having surveyed Gaussian processes, we return to Algorithm 1 and discuss the acquisition function used in that loop. The acquisition function “looks” at the Gaussian Process (also referred as Surrogate Model in general) and determines what areas are worth exploring and what areas are worth exploiting. Accordingly, in areas where \(f(x)\) is optimal or areas that we haven’t yet looked at, the acquisition function assumes a high value. On the contrary, in areas where \(f(x)\) is suboptimal or areas that we have already sampled from, AF’s value is small. By finding the \(x\) that maximizes the acquisition function, we identify the next best guess for \(f\) to try.

Instead of maximizing \(f\) directly whose analytic form is not known and its very expensive to evaluate, we maximize another function (acquisition function) which is much easier to do and much less expensive

There are multiple acquisition functions of course but we are going to only focus in only one which is one of the most common namely the Expected Improvement (EI).

Expected Improvement

Suppose that \(f^{*}\) is the maximum value that we have observed from our data so far. We are interested in quantifying the improvement over \(f^{*}\) we will have if we sample a point \(x\). Mathematically the improvement at \(x\) can be expressed as follows:

\[I(x) = \max(f(x) - f^{*}, 0)\]

where \(y = f(x) \sim \mathcal{N}(x^{\top}\mu_{0}, k(x,x) + \sigma_{k}^{2})\) or for brievity and for having easier calculations lets say that \(y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{f}, \sigma_{f}^{2})\).

Since \(I\) is a random variable (follows from \(y\) which is a random variable), one can consider the average (expected) improvement (EI) to assess \(x\):

\[\text{EI}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{f},\sigma_{f}^{2})}[I(x)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} I(x)\phi(y)dy = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\max(f^{*} - y, 0)\phi(y)dy\]

With the reparameterization trick, we can write \(y = \mu_{f} +\sigma_{f}z\) where \(z \sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Doing so, we can get rid of the \(\max\) operator by breaking the integral into 2 components, one where \(f^{*} - y\) is positive and one where it is negative. The breaking point is where \(y = \mu_{f} + \sigma_{f}z = f^{*}\) or \(z_{0} = (f^{*} - \mu_{f})/\sigma_{f}\).

\[\text{EI}(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{z_{0}}I(x)\phi(z)dz + \int_{z_{0}}^{\infty}I(x)\phi(z)dz = \int_{z_{0}}^{\infty}I(x)\phi(z)dz\]

since the first part zeroes out because \(I(x) = 0\) in that interval. In the interval \([z_{0}, \infty]\) we will have \(I(x) = f(x) - f^{*} = \mu_{f} - \sigma_{f}z - f^{*}\). Substituting to the equation above we will have

\[\begin{equation} \tag{5} \begin{split} \text{EI}(x) &= (\mu_{f} - f^{*})\int_{z_{0}}^{\infty}\phi(z)dz - \sigma_{f}\int_{z_{0}}^{\infty}z\phi(z)dz = (\mu_{f} - f^{*})\Phi(z_{0}) + \dfrac{\sigma_{f}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{z_{0}}z \exp(-z^{2} / 2) \\ &= (\mu_{f} - f^{*})\Phi(z_{0}) + \dfrac{\sigma_{f}}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp(-z^{2}/2) \Big |_{-\infty}^{z_{0}} \\ &= (\mu_{f} - f^{*})\Phi(z_{0}) + \sigma_{f}\phi(z_{0}) = (\mu_{f} - f^{*})\Phi\bigg(\dfrac{f^{*} - \mu_{f}}{\sigma_{f}}\bigg) + \sigma_{f}\phi\bigg(\dfrac{f^{*} - \mu_{f}}{\sigma_{f}}\bigg) \end{split} \end{equation}\]

where \(\Phi\) is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the standard normal distribution and \(\phi\) is the probability density function (PDF) of the standard normal distribution respectively. As we can see, we managed to get a closed form which is easy and quick to evaluate which is important for our purpose.

What we are interested in then is to find the next best point \(x_{k+1}\) that maximizes the Expected Improvement

\[x_{k+1} = \arg\max\text{EI}(x)\]

To do so, we can use a variety of implementation like first- or second- order optimization methods. Adam (as a first order optimization method) or even the quasi-Newton method L-BFGS work pretty well.

import torch.nn as nn
import math
from math import pi

inv_sqrt_2pi = (2 * pi) ** -0.5

def pdf(x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
    """Standard normal PDF."""
    return inv_sqrt_2pi * (-0.5 * x.square()).exp()

def cdf(x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
    """Standard normal CDF."""
    return torch.special.ndtr(x)

class ExpectedImprovement(nn.Module):

    def __init__(self, best_f: Tensor, gp: ExactGP):
        super().__init__()
        self.best_f = best_f
        self.gp = gp
    
    def forward(self, x):
        out = self.gp(x)
        mean, sigma = out.mean, out.stddev
        z = (mean - self.best_f) / sigma
        
        return sigma * pdf(z) + (mean - self.best_f) * cdf(z)

def maximize_eip(eip: ExpectedImprovement, 
                 num_iter: int, 
                 starting_point: Tensor | None) -> float:
    """Maximizes the Expected Improvement"""
    starting_point = starting_point or Tensor(.0, requires_grad=True)
    optimizer = torch.optim.Adam([starting_point], lr=0.1)

    for _ in range(num_iter):
        optimizer.zero_grad()
        loss = - eip(starting_point)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step() # update our starting point

    optimal_solution = starting_point.item()
    return optimal_solution

Putting it all together

Having defined all the necessary ingredients, we can go ahead and implement the bayesian optimization strategy that we saw before. Let’s start with a simple experiment assuming that we have a known function that we want to maximize \(f(x) = \sin(2 x\pi) + \varepsilon, \quad x \in [0,1], \space \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

In this case the linear kernel that we used before will not be sufficient since the \(\sin\) function is periodic and linear models cannot capture the periodicity. That’s why we are going to use the RBF Kernel defined as:

\[k(x, x') = \exp\bigg(-\dfrac{\Vert x - x' \Vert^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigg)\]

where \(\sigma\) is the lengthscale parameter. More information can be found on RBFKernel. As a mean function we can define \(m(x) = C, \space C \in \mathbb{R}\) since (in this particular example) we don’t expect any linear trend in the data. Additionally we will have to do some checks to assert that \(x \in [0,1]\).

class RBFGPModel(gpytorch.models.ExactGP):
    def __init__(self, train_x, train_y, likelihood):
        super(ExactGPModel, self).__init__(train_x, train_y, likelihood)
        self.mean_module = gpytorch.means.ConstantMean()
        self.covar_module = gpytorch.kernels.ScaleKernel(gpytorch.kernels.RBFKernel())

    def forward(self, x):
        mean_x = self.mean_module(x)
        covar_x = self.covar_module(x)
        return gpytorch.distributions.MultivariateNormal(mean_x, covar_x)

def f(x: Tensor) -> Tensor:
    """f(x) = sin(2 * pi * x) + e"""
    return (2 * pi * x).sin() + torch.rand(1)

def assert_x_bounded(x: Tensor, low: float = 0., high: float = 1.) -> bool:
    """assert x in [0,1]"""
    return (x > low and x < high).item()

### Bayesian Optimization
def bopt(n_iter: int = 30, 
         x_init: Tensor | None = None, 
         train_gp_iter: int = 100, 
         train_eip_iter: int = 100,
    ) -> Tensor:
    """Full bayesian optimization of our `black box` function"""
    x_init = x_init or torch.rand()
    
    x_collected = [x_init]
    y_collected = [f(x_init)]
    likelihood = gpytorch.likelihoods.GaussianLikelihood()

    for _ in range(n_iter):
        model = RBFGPModel(x_collected, y_collected, likelihood)
        train(model, likelihood, x_collected, y_collected, train_gp_iter)
        model.eval()

        best_f = model(x_collected).max()
        eip = ExpectedImprovement(best_f=best_f, gp=model)

        x_next = maximize_eip(eip, num_iter=train_eip_iter)
        while not assert_x_bounded(x_next): # propose next point that is bounded to [0, 1]
            x_next = maximize_eip(eip, num_iter=train_eip_iter) # start again

        x_collected.append(x_next)
        y_collected.append(f(x_next)) # evaluate to our f

    return torch.Tensor(y_collected).max()

Running the code above, we can also plot how our bayesian optimization progresses while we collect more data and our Gaussian process updates its estimates

As we can see, by adding more data our Gaussian process quickly becomes more confident about its estimates and our acquisition function can identify where we should evaluate next. By the end of our process we can see that the bayesian optimization was able to find the maximum of our function.

Appendix

Definition 1. (Likelihood of Multivariate Normal)
The probability density function of a multivariate normal distribution of a k-dimensional random vector \(x = [x_{1}, \ldots, x_{k}]^{\top} \) is defined as $$ (2\pi)^{-k/2} |\Sigma|^{-1/2}\exp\bigg(-\dfrac{1}{2}(x - \mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\bigg) $$ Accordingly, our log likelihood function is given by $$ \ln L(\mu, \Sigma, x) = -\dfrac{1}{2}\big[\ln\big(|\Sigma|\big) + (x - \mu^{\top})\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu) + k \ln(2\pi)\big] $$


Theorem 1. (Conditional Gaussian). Let \(x\) follow a multivariate normal distribution \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\) Then, the conditional distribution of any subset vector \(x_1\), given the complement vector \(x_2\), is also a multivariate normal distribution $$ x_{1} | x_{2} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{1|2}, \Sigma_{1 | 2}) $$ where the conditional mean and covariance are $$ \begin{split} \mu_{1|2} &= \mu_{1} + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_{2} - \mu_{2})\\ \Sigma_{1|2} &= \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \end{split} $$ with block-wise mean and covariance defined as $$ \mu = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_1 \\ \mu_2 \end{bmatrix} \quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22} \end{bmatrix} $$


PROOF.

There are multiple proofs for the conditional Gaussian, but we are going to show the shortest one. For a secondary proof check out this one: Multivariate Conditional.

The core idea is to construct a new random variable that is a linear transformation of \(x\) and is uncorrelated with \(x_{2}\). We start by defining \(z = x_{1} + Ax_{2}\), where \(A\) is a matrix we will determine. The mean of \(z\) is

\[\mathbb{E}[z] = \mathbb{E}[x_{1} + Ax_{2}] = \mathbb{E}[x_{1}] + \mathbb{E}[Ax_{2}] = \mu_{1} + A\mu_{2}.\]

The covariance between \(z\) and \(x_{2}\) is:

\[\text{cov}(z, x_{2}) = \text{cov}(x_{1} + Ax_{2}, x_{2}) = \text{cov}(x_{1}, x_{2}) + \text{cov}(Ax_{2}, x_{2}) = \Sigma_{12} + A\Sigma_{22}.\]

To make \(z\) and \(x_{2}\) uncorrelated, we need to set their covariance to zero:

\[\Sigma_{12} + A\Sigma_{22} = 0 \Rightarrow A = - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\]

Now, let’s define \(z\) as \(z = x_{1} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}x_{2}\). Since \(z\) and \(x_{2}\) are uncorrelated and jointly normally distributed, they are independent. This means that conditioning on \(x_{2} = a\) does not change the distribution of \(z\).

Therefore

\[\begin{split} \mu_{1 | 2} &= \mathbb{E}[x_{1} | x_{2}] = \mathbb{E}[z - Ax_{2} | x_{2}] = \mathbb{E}[z | x_{2}] - \mathbb{E}[Ax_{2} | x_{2}] = \mathbb{E}[z] - Ax_{2} = \mu_{1} + A\mu_{2} - Ax_{2} = \mu_{1} + A(\mu_{2} - x_{2}) \\ &= \mu_{1} + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_{2} - \mu_{2}) \end{split}\]

And

\[\begin{split} \Sigma_{1|2} &= \text{Var}(z - Ax_{2} | x_{2}) = \text{Var}(z | x_{2}) = \text{Var}(z) = \text{Var}(x_{1} + Ax_{2}) = \text{Var}(x_{1}) + A\text{Var}(x_{2})A^{\top} + A\text{Cov}(x_{1}, x_{2}) + A\text{Cov}(x_{2}, x_{1})\\ &= \Sigma_{11} + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{22}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{12} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \quad {\tiny \blacksquare} \end{split}\]
Theorem 2. (Linear combination of independent normals). Let \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) be independent normally distributed random variables with means \(\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_n\) and variances \(\sigma^2_1, \ldots, \sigma^2_n\): $$ \label{eq:norm} x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i) \quad \text{for} \quad i = 1, \ldots, n \; . $$ Then, any linear combination of those random variables $$ \label{eq:lincomb} y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i x_i \quad \text{where} \quad a_1, \ldots, a_n \in \mathbb{R} $$ also follows a normal distribution $$ \label{eq:norm-lincomb} y \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mu_i, \; \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^2 \sigma^2_i \right) $$ with mean and variance which are functions of the individual means and variances.


PROOF.

A set of $n$ independent normal random variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ is equivalent to an $n \times 1$ random vector $x$ following a multivariate normal distribution with a diagonal covariance matrix. Therefore, we can write

\[\label{eq:norm-mvn} x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i), \; i = 1, \ldots, n \quad \Rightarrow \quad x = \left[ \begin{array}{c} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{array} \right] \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\]

with mean vector and covariance matrix

\[\label{eq:mu-Sigma} \mu = \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mu_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mu_n \end{array} \right] \quad \text{and} \quad \Sigma = \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} \sigma^2_1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & \sigma^2_n \end{array} \right] = \mathrm{diag}\left( \left[ \sigma^2_1, \ldots, \sigma^2_n \right] \right) \; .\]

Thus, we can apply the linear transformation theorem for the multivariate normal distribution

\[\label{eq:mvn-ltt} x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma) \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = Ax + b \sim \mathcal{N}(A\mu + b, A \Sigma A^{\top})\]

with the constant matrix and vector

\[\label{eq:A-b} A = \left[ a_1, \ldots, a_n \right] \quad \text{and} \quad b = 0 \; .\]

This implies the following distribution for the linear combination given by the equation above:

\[\label{eq:norm-lincomb-p1} y = Ax + b \sim \mathcal{N}(A\mu, A \Sigma A^{\top}) \; .\]

Finally, we note that

\[\label{eq:A-b-mu-Sigma} \begin{split} A \mu &= \left[ a_1, \ldots, a_n \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} \mu_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mu_n \end{array} \right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \mu_i \quad \text{and} \quad \\ A \Sigma A^{\top} &= \left[ a_1, \ldots, a_n \right] \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} \sigma^2_1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & \sigma^2_n \end{array} \right] \left[ \begin{array}{c} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{array} \right] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^2 \sigma^2_i \; \quad {\tiny \blacksquare} \end{split}\]
