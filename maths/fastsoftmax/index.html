In this post, I’ll discuss about a very delicate optimization that I came across based on a function that we are all familiar with – the softmax function.

What is Softmax?

It’s very natural to ask ourselves how did the softmax function came up and why this is the de-facto choice for multiclass classification.

Consider a classification problem in which a variable \(y\) can take on any one of \(k\) values, so \(y \in \{1,2,\ldots, k\}\). For example, rather than classifying an email into the two classes spam or not-spam — which would have been a binary classification problem — we might want to classify it into three classes, such as spam, personal email, and work-related email. The variable \(y\) is still discrete, but can now take on more than two values. We will thus model it as distributed according to a multinomial distribution (keep this in mind because the softmax function arises naturally from the definition of the Generalized Linear Model).

But let’s see first what is a GLM and how we can construct one.

Generalized Linear Models

We begin by defining exponential family distributions. We say that a class of distributions is in the exponential family if it can be written in the form

\[p(y ; \eta) = b(y)\exp(\eta^{\top}T(y) - a(\eta))\]

Here, \(\eta\) is called the natural parameter; \(T(y)\) is the sufficient statistic; and \(a(\eta)\) is the log partition function. The quantity \(e^{-a(\eta)}\) essentially plays the role of a normalization constant, that makes sure the distribution \(p(y;\eta)\) sums over \(y\) to \(1\).

We can show that the Bernoulli and the Gaussian distributions are examples of exponential family distributions (ordinary least squares is too but we won’t show that). We can, for example write the Bernoulli distribution as:

\[\begin{split} p(y;\phi) &= \phi^{y}(1-\phi)^{1-y} \\ &= \exp(y\log \phi + (1-y)\log(1-\phi)) \\ &= \exp\bigg( \bigg( \log \bigg( \dfrac{\phi}{1-\phi} \bigg) \bigg) \bigg) \end{split}\]

Thus, the natural parameter is given by \(\eta = \log(\phi /(1-\phi))\). Interestingly, if we invert this definition for \(\eta\) by solving for \(\phi\) in terms of \(\eta\), we obtain \(\phi = 1 / (1 + e^{-\eta})\) which is our beloved sigmoid function. So as we see here, the sigmoid function arises naturally for the case of the binary classification where \(y \in \{0,1\}\) and \(p(y = 1;\eta) = \phi\).

Softmax Regression

Now, let’s look how we can derive a GLM for modelling the multinomial data. To do so, we will begin by expressing the multinomial as an exponential family distribution. To parameterize a multinomial over \(k\) possible outcomes, one could use \(k\) parameters \(\phi_{1},\ldots \phi_{k}\) specifying the probability of each of the outcomes. However, these parameters would not be independent since knowing any \(k-1\) of the \(\phi_{i}\)’s uniquely determines the last one, as the must satisfy \(\sum_{i=1}^{k} \phi_{i} = 1\).

So, we will instead parameterize the multinomial with only \(k-1\) parameters, \(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k-1}\), where \(\phi_{i} = p(y = i, \phi)\) and \(p(y = k; \phi) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k} \phi_{i} = 1\). For notational convenience, we will also let \(\phi_{k} = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k} \phi_{i}\), but this is not a parameter as it is fully specified by \(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k-1}\).

To express the multinomial as an exponential family distribution, we will define \(T(y)\in \mathbb{R}^{k-1}\) as follows:

\[T(1) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T(2) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T(3) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \cdots, \quad T(k-1) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad T(k) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\]

\(T(y)\) is now a \(k-1\) dimensional vector, rather than a real number. We will write \((T(y))_{i}\) to denote the \(i\)-th element of the vector \(T(y)\). We introduce one more very useful piece of notation. An indicator function \(1\{\cdot\}\) takes on a value of \(1\) if its argument its true, and \(0\) otherwise. So, we can write the relationship between \(T(y)\) and \(y\) as \((T(y))_{i} = 1\{y = i\}\). For the multinomial distribution, we will then have:

\[\begin{split} p(y;\phi) &= \phi_{1}^{1\{y=1\}}\phi_{2}^{1\{y=2\}}\cdots\phi_{k}^{1\{y=k\}}\\ &= \phi_{1}^{1\{y=1\}}\phi_{2}^{1\{y=2\}}\cdots\phi_{k}^{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}1\{y=i\}}\\ &= \phi_{1}^{(T(y))_{1}}\phi_{2}^{(T(y))_{2}}\cdots\phi_{k}^{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}(T(y))_{i}}\\ &= \exp\bigg((T(y))_{1}\log(\phi_{1}) + (T(y))_{2}\log(\phi_{2}) + \cdots + \big(1 - \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}T(y)_{i}\big)\log(\phi_{k})\bigg) \\ &= \exp\bigg((T(y))_{1}\log(\phi_{1}/\phi_{k}) + (T(y))_{2}\log(\phi_{2}/\phi_{k}) + \cdots + (T(y))_{k-1}\log(\phi_{k-1}/\phi_{k}) + \log(\phi_{k})\bigg) \\ &= b(y)\exp\big(\eta^{\top}T(y) - a(\eta)\big) \end{split}\]

where \(\eta = \begin{bmatrix} \log\left(\frac{\phi_1}{\phi_k}\right) \\ \log\left(\frac{\phi_2}{\phi_k}\right) \\ \vdots \\ \log\left(\frac{\phi_{k-1}}{\phi_k}\right) \end{bmatrix} , \quad a(\eta) = -\log(\phi_k), \quad b(y) = 1.\)

This completes our formulation of the multinomial as an exponential family distribution. We can observe that the link function is given (for \(i=1,\ldots,k\)) by \(\eta_{i} = \log\big(\dfrac{\phi_{i}}{\phi_{k}}\big)\). By inverting the link function we end up to the response function which is given by:

\[\phi_{i} = \dfrac{e^{\eta_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{\eta_{j}}}\]

which is called the softmax function!

To make it easier to understand the implementations below, we will change the formulation a bit and we will write:

\[y_{i} = \text{Softmax}(x_{i}) = \dfrac{e^{x_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{x_{j}}}\]

Implementations

Naive Softmax

Now that we saw how softmax is derived, we will explore how we use it. In most Deep Learning applications, softmax is used as the final layer of a neural network to predict the class of our multiclass classification problem (we will how it is used in Attention). But in order to use it efficiently, we need to have a GPU kernel that is fast and can be used in Deep Learning applications.

The basic algorithm for calculating the softmax function over an input \(x\) is depicted below:

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Naive Softmax calculation}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \(e_{0} = 0 \).
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(e_{k} = e_{k-1} + \) exp\((x_{k})\).
\EndFor
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(y_{k} = \) exp \((x_{k}) / e_{T}\).
\EndFor
\STATE Return vector \(y\).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Safe Softmax

Unfortunately, on real hardware, where the range of numbers represented is limited, the line 3 of the algorithm 1 can overflow or underflow due to the exponent. There is a safe form, which is immune to this problem:

\[y_{i} = \dfrac{e^{x_{i} - \max_{j=1}^{k}x_{j}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{x_{j} - \max_{l=1}^{k}x_{l}}}\]

Essentially, what we do is to subtract \(\max_{k=1}^{V} x_{k}\) from all $x_{k}$ in order to avoid overflow (because overflow might occur from big numbers that get exponentiated). All major DL frameworks are using this safe version for the Softmax computation.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Safe Softmax calculation}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \(e_{0} = - \) INFINITY.
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(e_{k} = \) max\((e_{k-1}, x_{k})\).
\EndFor
\STATE \(a_{0} = 0\)
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(a_{k} = a_{k-1} + \) exp \((x_{k} - e_{T})\).
\EndFor
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(y_{k} = \) exp \((x_{k} - e_{T}) / a_{T}\).
\EndFor
\STATE Return vector \(y\).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

template <int ILP, typename scalar_t, typename accscalar_t, typename outscalar_t, template <typename, typename, typename> class Epilogue>
__global__ void
cunn_SoftMaxForward(outscalar_t *output, const scalar_t *input, int classes)
{
  extern __shared__ unsigned char smem[];
  auto sdata = reinterpret_cast<accscalar_t*>(smem);

  // forward pointers to batch[blockIdx.x]
  // each block handles a sample in the mini-batch
  input += static_cast<int64_t>(blockIdx.x) * classes;
  output += static_cast<int64_t>(blockIdx.x) * classes;

  const int shift = ((uint64_t)input) % ALIGN_BYTES / sizeof(scalar_t);
  const int output_shift = ((uint64_t)output) % ALIGN_BYTES / sizeof(outscalar_t);

  // find the max
  accscalar_t threadMax = ilpReduce<MaxFloat, ILP, scalar_t, accscalar_t>(
    shift, input, classes, MaxFloat<scalar_t, accscalar_t>(), -at::numeric_limits<accscalar_t>::max());
  accscalar_t max_k = blockReduceWarp<Max, accscalar_t>(sdata, threadMax,
    Max<accscalar_t>(), -at::numeric_limits<accscalar_t>::max());

  // reduce all values
  accscalar_t threadExp = ilpReduce<SumExpFloat, ILP, scalar_t, accscalar_t>(
    shift, input, classes, SumExpFloat<scalar_t, accscalar_t>(max_k), static_cast<accscalar_t>(0));
  accscalar_t sumAll = blockReduceWarp<Add, accscalar_t>(sdata, threadExp,
    Add<accscalar_t>(), static_cast<accscalar_t>(0));

  Epilogue<scalar_t, accscalar_t, outscalar_t> epilogue(max_k, sumAll);

  if (shift == output_shift) {
    WriteFpropResultsVectorized<ILP, scalar_t, accscalar_t, outscalar_t, Epilogue>(classes, shift, input, output, epilogue);
  } else {
    WriteFpropResults<ILP, scalar_t, accscalar_t, outscalar_t, Epilogue>(classes, input, output, epilogue);
  }
}

There are couple things here defined so let’s start to decouple them a bit by focusing more on the important stuff.

  • Lines 5-6 create a dynamically sized block of shared memory for faster access. Shared memory is accessible by all threads within the block and its size is varied in every GPU. auto sdata = ... reinterprets the raw byte array smem as a pointer to the accumulation type accscalar_t. sdata will be used in reduction operations (like max) to avoid reading/writing data from VRAM and instead use shared memory.

  • Lines 10-11 adjust the global input and output pointers so that each block advances its pointers to the start of the specific sample it’s responsible for processing in the mini batch.

  • Lines 13-14 are not particularly interesting and they calculate the memory alignment offset. If the input and the output have the same alignment offset, then a faster vectorized instruction can be used (WriteFPropResultsVectorized) for writing data with higher memory bandwidth (Lines 30-33).

  • Line 17 performs the first part of the max reduction. Every thread is responsible for finding the max in a subset of the data. Line 18 uses the results of the previous work to complete the reduction. Every thread in the block uses warp level instructions to compute the local max and shared memory for read/write and to calculate the overall maximum.

  • Lines 23-26 perform exactly the same thing as previously only now to calculate the sum of the exponentials.

  • Finally at Line 28, the Epilogue is responsible for the last loop in Algorithm 2 — that is to subtract every value of the original vector with the max and divide it by the sum that was calculated previously.

Online Softmax

And here we finally arrived to the final bit of this blogpost. In our previous algorithm (Algorithm 2) Safe Softmax did three passes over the input vector. The first one calculates the maximum value \(e_{T}\), the second one – normalization term \(a_{T}\), and the third one – final values \(y_{k}\). This results in 4 memory accesses per vector element overall.

Online normalizer calculation

Our goal is to improve on the memory accesses in order to speed up the kernel that we saw above. The following algorthm does exactly that.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Online Softmax calculation}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \(e_{0} = - \) INFINITY.
\STATE \(a_{0} = 0\)
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(e_{k} = \) max\((e_{k-1}, x_{k})\).
    \STATE \(a_{k} = a_{k-1} \cdot\) exp\((e_{k-1} - e_{k}) + \) exp\((x_{k}-e_{k})\) 
\EndFor
\For {\(k = 1 ... T\)}
    \STATE \(y_{k} = \) exp \((x_{k} - e_{T}) / a_{T}\).
\EndFor
\STATE Return vector \(y\).
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Essentially, the algorithm keeps the maximum value \(e\) and the normalization term \(a\) as it iterates over elements of the input array. At each iteration it needs to adjust the normalizer \(a\) to the new maximum \(e_{k}\) and only then add new value to the normalizer.

If a new max is found, the algorithm makes sure to add a correction term \(\exp(e_{k-1} - e_{k})\) to the previous sum and then add the new term. If a new maximum is not found then the first term \(a_{k-1}\cdot \exp(e_{k-1} - e_{k})\) becomes \(a_{k-1}\) and the calculation is the same as with the previous version we saw. The proof that this algorithm calculates the softmax correctly can be found in the Appendix.

Let’s see it with an example:

\[\begin{align*} &x = [1, 2, 3] \\ & \\ &\text{Step 1} \\ &\rule{14cm}{0.6pt} \\ &e_0 = -\infty \\ &a_0 = 0 \\ &e_1 = x_1 = 1 \\ &a_1 = a_0 \cdot e^{e_1 - e_0} + e^{x_1 - e_1} = 0 \cdot e^{1 - (-\infty)} + e^{1-1} = e^0 = 1 \\[1em] &\text{Step 2 (New max is found!)} \space \\ &\rule{14cm}{0.6pt} \\ &e_2 = \max(e_{1}, x_2) = \max(1, 2) = 2 \\ &a_2 = a_1 \cdot e^{e_1 - e_2} + e^{x_2 - e_2} = 1 \cdot e^{1-2} + e^{2-2} = e^{-1} + 1 \\[1em] &\text{Step 3 (New max is found!)} \\ &\rule{14cm}{0.6pt} \\ &e_3 = \max(e_{2}, x_3) = \max(2, 3) = 3 \\ &a_3 = a_2 \cdot e^{e_2 - e_3} + e^{x_3 - e_3} = (e^{-1} + 1) \cdot e^{2-3} + e^{3-3} = e^{-2} + e^{-1} + 1 \end{align*}\]

You can observe that if we new from the beginning that \(\max(x) = 3\) and we subtracted \(3\) from every term of \(x\) and then take the \(\exp\) and sum of all the elements, we would end up with \(e^{-2} + e^{-1} + 1\) which is exactly what we ended up with the algorithm above.

Parallel online normalizer calculation

To fully utilize the device, we will need a parallel version of the algorithm for which we will use the following definition of a generalized version of the online normalizer calculation:

\[\begin{bmatrix} e_T \\ a_T \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \oplus \begin{bmatrix} x_2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \oplus \ldots \oplus \begin{bmatrix} x_T \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \tag 1\]

where $x_i, e_T, a_T \in \mathbb{R}$. The binary operation $\oplus: \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^2$ is defined as:

\[\begin{bmatrix} e_i \\ a_i \end{bmatrix} \oplus \begin{bmatrix} e_j \\ a_j \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \max(e_i, e_j) \\ a_i \cdot \exp(e_i - \max(e_i, e_j)) + a_j\cdot \exp(e_j - \max(e_i,e_j)) \end{bmatrix}\]

Applying the (1) sequentially from left to right is equivalent to running lines 1-6 of the Αlgorithm 3. The operation $\oplus$ is associative, which enables parallel evaluation of (1). It is also commutative, which provides the flexibility needed to make parallel implementations more efficient. We omit the proofs for these two statements for brevity.

// We need to hold the max and the normalizer to apply the operator. Align the struct to apply vector instructions (__expf)
struct __align__(8) MaxNorm {
  float m;
  float d;
};

// The oplus operator that we defined above
__device__ __forceinline__ MaxNorm reduce_md_op(MaxNorm a, MaxNorm b) {
    bool a_bigger = (a.m > b.m);
    MaxNorm bigger_m = a_bigger ? a : b;
    MaxNorm smaller_m = a_bigger ? b : a;
    MaxNorm res;
    res.d = bigger_m.d + smaller_m.d * __expf(smaller_m.m - bigger_m.m);
    res.m = bigger_m.m;
    return res;
}

template<int THREADBLOCK_SIZE>
__launch_bounds__(THREADBLOCK_SIZE)
__global__ void
cu_online_softmax(float * __restrict output, const float * __restrict input, int classes) {

  //  forward pointers to batch[blockIdx.x]
  // each block handles a sample in the mini-batch
  input += blockIdx.x * classes;
  output += blockIdx.x * classes;

  typedef cub::BlockReduce<MaxNorm, THREADBLOCK_SIZE> BlockReduce;

  __shared__ typename BlockReduce::TempStorage smem;
  __shared__ MaxNorm sumAll;

  MaxNorm sumPartial;
  sumPartial.m = -FLT_MAX;
  sumPartial.d = 0.0F;

  // Partial max-sum
  #pragma unroll
  for(int offset = threadIdx.x; offset < classes; offset += THREADBLOCK_SIZE) {
    MaxNorm threadMaxNorm;
    threadMaxNorm.m = input[offset];
    threadMaxNorm.d = 1.0F;
    sumPartial = reduce_md_op(sumPartial, threadMaxNorm);
  }

  // Reduce from each block
  MaxNorm blockReduceMaxNorm = BlockReduce(smem).Reduce(sumPartial, reduce_md_op);
  if (threadIdx.x == 0)
    sumAll = blockReduceMaxNorm;
  __syncthreads();

  // Same Epilogue as previously
  float sumAllInverse = __fdividef(1.0F, sumAll.d);
  for(int offset = threadIdx.x; offset < classes; offset += THREADBLOCK_SIZE)
    output[offset] = __expf(input[offset] - sumAll.m) * sumAllInverse;
}

Let’s analyze the code above:

  • Lines (8-16) define the binary operation \(\oplus: \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}^2\) that we saw previously. __device__ specifies that this function will be compiled for the GPU and __inline__ specifier is used to hint the compiler to inline the function into the kernel’s body (avoid overhead of a function call which is critical when executing parallel code).
  • Lines (25-31) as previously adjust the global input and output pointers, create the BlockReduce object that will use the binary operation to perform the reduction and create the shared memory smem that will be used from this BlockReduce object to facilitate the communication between threads.
  • Lines (33-44) perform the first partial reduction. Again, as previously each thread is responsible for calculating the max and the normalizer in a subset of data. #pragma unroll is used to for loop unrolling in order to improve the performance and avoid unnecessary branching.
  • Lines (47-49) perform the block reduction using the warp level instructions as before. In Line 50 we use __syncthreads() to make sure that every thread has finished its work before calculating the iverse of the overall sum.
  • Finally, in Lines (53-55) we first calculate the inverse of the overall sum using fast approximate intrinsics and then finally we calculate the softmax of every value.

Appendix

Theorem 1. The lines 1-6 of algorithm 3 compute \(e_{T} = \max_{k=1}^{T}x_{k}\) and \(a_{T} = \sum_{k=1}^{T}\exp(x_{k}-e_{T})\).


PROOF.

We will use a proof by induction.

\[\begin{align*} &\space & & \lozenge \space \textbf{Base case:} \quad V = 1 \\ e_1 &\leftarrow x_1 & &\text{by line 4 of the algorithm 3} \\ &= \max_{k=1}^1 x_k \\ a_1 &\leftarrow \exp(x_1 - m_1) & &\text{by line 5 of the algorithm 3} \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^1 \exp(x_k - m_1) \end{align*}\]

The theorem holds for $V = 1$. We assume the theorem statement holds for \(V = T-1\), that is the lines 1-6 of the algorithm 3 compute \(e_{T-1} = \max_{k=1}^{T-1} x_{k}\) and \(a_{T-1} = \sum_{k=1}^{T-1} \exp(x_{k} - e_{T-1})\). Let’s see what the algorithm computes for \(V = T\).

\[\begin{align*} &\space & & \lozenge \space \textbf{Inductive step}\\ m_T &\leftarrow \max (m_{T-1}, x_T) & &\text{by line 4 of the algorithm 3} \\ &= \max\left(\max_{k=1}^{T-1} x_k, x_T\right) & &\text{by the inductive hypothesis} \\ &= \max_{k=1}^T x_k \\ a_T &\leftarrow a_{T-1} \times \exp(e_{T-1} - e_T) + \exp(x_T - e_T) & &\text{by line 5 of the algorithm 3} \\ &= \left(\sum_{k=1}^{T-1} \exp(x_k - e_{T-1})\right) \times \exp(e_{T-1} - m_T) + \exp(x_T - e_T) & &\text{by the inductive hypothesis} \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^{T-1} \exp(x_k - e_T) + \exp(x_T - e_T) \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^T \exp(x_k - e_T) \quad {\tiny \blacksquare} \end{align*}\]
